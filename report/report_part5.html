5. Explainability (XAI)

Explanations are not causal effects. A feature being important means the model relies on it to predict. It does not mean the feature causes podiums. For example, con_pts_share_before is important because strong teams both score points and finish on the podium; giving a team points would not make its cars faster. Correlated features also share importance. Every statement in this section describes the models' behaviour, not the sport.

5.1 What we explain, and on which data

We explain all five models, leading with the recommended one, the logistic regression (Section 4.5); the other four serve as a cross-check of whether very different models rely on the same information. All explanations are computed on the unseen test seasons (2022–2024); explaining does not choose anything, so this does not leak.

  • Global: permutation importance per feature and per feature group; SHAP summary (beeswarm) and mean |SHAP| per model.
  • Local: a SHAP waterfall and LIME for the same single prediction, plus each model's most confident wrong prediction.
  • SHAP set-up. One model-agnostic PermutationExplainer for all five models, in probability units, on the original feature values. The background ("a typical driver") is 100 training rows from 2003–2019, the seasons with complete qualifying data, like every row we explain (D26). For every explained row the base value plus the contributions equals the model's probability (largest gap 1e-8).

5.4 Local SHAP: one prediction explained

We explain a well-known test case: Max Verstappen at the 2023 Belgian GP. He was fastest in qualifying, took a 5-place gearbox penalty, started 6th, and won. The models' most confident mistakes

For the logistic regression, the forest and the network, the highest probability given to a driver who did not podium is the same row: Verstappen at the 2024 Australian GP, on pole and leading both championships (LR 0.849). He retired with a brake failure. For the LR every group pushed up (grid +0.313, form +0.139, standings +0.118, qualifying +0.101). Nothing in the pre-race data could have announced a component failure: the models rank the field well but cannot foresee breakdowns or crashes (Section 4.7).

5.5 LIME for the same prediction

LIME creates perturbed copies of the row, asks the model about each, and fits a small weighted linear model around that point. It describes features by ranges (e.g. grid_pos ≤ 6), changes features independently (so some synthetic rows are impossible), and is random, so we ran it with 5 seeds.

Table 5.6 LIME weights for the logistic regression, averaged over 5 seeds. Local fit R² 0.69–0.70; LIME's own prediction 0.28 against the model's 0.735.

  • Direction agrees with SHAP. For every model grid_pos ≤ 6 is the largest weight in all 5 seeds, followed by a qualifying or standings condition with the same sign SHAP gave.
  • But LIME fits this point only approximately. Its R² is 0.70 for the logistic regression and 0.34–0.52 for the forest, default gradient boosting and the network, and its own prediction (0.28 for the LR) is far from the model's. LIME draws synthetic rows from the whole training distribution, mostly midfield cars with near-zero podium chances, and one straight line through them cannot follow the steep rise near this row. (Tuned gradient boosting fits best, R² 0.88, only because its output barely moves.)
  • SHAP is therefore the main local explanation: it adds up exactly to each model's output.

5.6 Summary of the explanations

  • All methods and all five models agree that starting position is what the models rely on most, followed by season-long strength (standings) and recent form; the logistic regression also uses qualifying pace.
  • Group-level views are the trustworthy ones: correlated features share and trade importance.
  • The local explanations show what the models get right (a penalised pole-sitter in the dominant car is still a likely podium) and what they cannot know (a brake failure from pole).
  • These explain the models' reasoning, not what causes a podium.
